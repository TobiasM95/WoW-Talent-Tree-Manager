Counting every build without listing one

One Resto Druid spec tree has 305,286,987 valid builds. Visiting each one takes 42 seconds. Counting all of them takes 52,421 additions — and not because any build is skipped or estimated. Every single one is counted. They are just counted in groups.

First: this is not an approximation

Sum the numbers 1 to 100. You can add a hundred numbers, or you can notice they pair up — 1+100, 2+99, … — and do 50 × 101 = 5,050. The second way skips nothing. Every number is in the answer. It just exploits the fact that the numbers come in interchangeable pairs.

Counting talent builds works the same way, and the classic example of the machinery is a grid. How many routes cross this grid, moving only right or down? You could walk each route. Or you could write, at every intersection, the number of routes that reach it — (the number above) + (the number to the left) — and read the last one.

That last sentence is the whole idea. A talent tree is the same trick with a richer notion of "intersection", and the rest of this page is about what that intersection has to be.

What plays the part of a cell, and of its two neighbours

The natural guess is that a talent is a cell and its parents are the neighbours. It is worth saying plainly: neither of those is right. The cells are not talents at all, and the arrows do not run along the tree's edges.

So points spent is one coordinate of the cell, and the remembered talents are the other. Every state has at most two arrows into it, exactly as in the grid. The tree's own edges decide only whether an arrow is legal — they are the walls, not the routes.

Why the point barriers still work

This is the part that looks impossible: if the sweep forgets the past, how can it know whether enough points were spent to pass a gate? Because points spent is not part of the past — it is part of the state, carried forward at every step:

state = ( which remembered talents are taken , points spent so far )  →  how many builds reach it

A gate asks only how many points have been spent, never which talents they went into. So two different selections that agree on both halves of that key are interchangeable for every future decision — and can be added together. That addition is the whole saving.

Where the speed actually comes from

Read the Builds column above. A row reading 4,000,000 means four million distinct builds are sitting in that one row, because they are indistinguishable with respect to every decision still to come.

Deciding the next talent for all four million of them is one addition. The enumerator holds those four million as four million separate objects and makes that same decision four million times. That difference, compounded over the whole tree, is the entire speedup — not fewer builds, just fewer decisions per build.

The same sweep on a real spec tree

Measured on druid_restoration — 43 talents, 48 point slots, gates at 8 and 20 points. The sweep never carries more than a few thousand states, however many builds exist below it.

Valid builds at 30 points 305,286,987 Each one a separate visit for the enumerator.
Peak states carried 2,427 The most the sweep ever holds at once.
Enumerate, then count 42.0 s One budget, one tree, C++ release build.
Sweep, all 78 presets 3.7 s Every budget of every class and spec tree, in Python.
Both implementations agree exactly where they can both be run: 223 / 17,541 / 71,030 / 305,286,987 builds on druid_restoration at 10 / 15 / 20 / 30 points, and on 11 further trees across 6 classes. The sweep also predicted 3,325,013,320 builds for shaman_class_elemental in 0.2 s — a number the enumerator needed 281 s to confirm, and which used to overflow its 32-bit counter. Enumeration is still the right tool for listing builds that match a filter; it just should not be the one answering “how many”.
